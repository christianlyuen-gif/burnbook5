Learning from history: what Apollo 1 and Apollo 13 taught

Apollo 1 and Apollo 13 were tragic accidents caused by oversights in cabin oxygen level, materials and flamibility. Burnbook has gathered the improvements that NASA has made since these accidents, and data to explain how these incidents occured. .

What fire looks like in orbit

Without gravity there is no rising hot air, so flames take the shape of the airflow instead, or grow into spheres when there is no flow at all. Crew photos from BASS and BASS-II and camera frames from ACME.

Soot score

Blue light comes from the reaction zone; yellow, orange and white light is glowing soot. The score is the share of flame pixels in soot colours, from 0 (all blue) to 1 (all soot). It is measured by image processing, so the same photo always gives the same number. It counts area, not brightness, so a dim soot glow that fills a flame still scores high.

    Ground tests understate the hazard

    NASA's standard upward-burn test is used to screen materials for spacecraft. In microgravity, all three fuels tested burned at lower oxygen than that test predicts.

    More oxygen, longer-lived flames

    CFI-G lit small gas flames on a spherical burner in still air. Near ordinary 21% oxygen every flame went out on its own within seconds; at the 30 to 40% oxygen planned for some exploration cabins, many were still burning minutes later when the fuel was shut off.

    Went out on its ownStill burning when the fuel was cut (lasted longer)Your cabin oxygen (from the scenario above)

    Suppressant gases cut droplet fires short

    FLEX burned fuel droplets on the ISS in still air. Each mark is one droplet: how much of it burned before its flame went out. Near the oxygen limit (13 to 16%), droplets in air diluted with CO₂ or helium burned less than droplets in nitrogen alone. Bigger marks are bigger droplets, which also go out sooner.

    Flammability boundary: too little or too much air flow puts a flame out

    For PMMA rods burning in the ISS and in drop towers, the oxygen needed to keep a flame going is lowest at moderate flow. Slow flow starves the flame (quench); fast flow blows it off.
